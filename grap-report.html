Abstract

Delhi-NCR experiences recurrent winter episodes of severe air pollution. The Graded Response Action Plan (GRAP) is Delhi-NCR's emergency response framework, under which pollution-control measures escalate as air quality deteriorates. This study evaluates whether exposure to GRAP Stage III/IV was associated with a reduction in PM2.5 concentrations.

Unlike simple before-and-after comparisons, this study corrects for uncertain GRAP implementation timings, overlapping exposure windows, repeated entries into stricter stages, and contamination of comparison periods. It also accounts for the fact that pollution observations are clustered within episodes. The analysis uses an episode-level moving-block bootstrap, placebo-in-time testing, sign-flip testing, leave-one-episode-out validation and multiple sensitivity analyses.

In the week after Stage III/IV began, PM2.5 changed by +11.7% (1+PM2.5 scale) relative to the change expected after comparable unexposed days (95% CI: −9.4% to +25.5%). Because the interval includes zero, the study finds no statistically significant reduction in PM2.5 associated with Stage III/IV exposure.

The positive point estimate should not be read as evidence that GRAP increased PM2.5; it is consistent with confounding by indication, since stricter stages are activated when air quality is already severe. Severe pollution also persisted outside Stage III/IV: 102 of 249 severe station-days occurred when Stage III/IV was not in force.

01Introduction

Air pollution is one of Delhi-NCR's most persistent environmental and public-health challenges. Winter months are particularly severe because low wind speeds, reduced atmospheric mixing, temperature inversions and regional pollution transport trap pollutants close to the surface. PM2.5 is of special concern because these fine particles penetrate deep into the respiratory system and are strongly associated with adverse health outcomes.

The Graded Response Action Plan was introduced as an emergency framework to respond to deteriorating air quality. Under GRAP, restrictions escalate across stages as AQI worsens. Stage III and Stage IV are the stricter tiers and are typically activated during poor-to-severe pollution episodes.

A central difficulty in evaluating GRAP is that it is not randomly implemented: stricter stages are triggered in response to high pollution. A simple comparison of air quality before and after activation can therefore mislead, because pollution may already be high when GRAP begins and may stay high because of meteorology, regional emissions, festivals, stubble burning or other interventions.

This study addresses that challenge with a corrected, episode-based evaluation. It asks whether clean, comparable episodes of Stage III/IV exposure show evidence of a PM2.5 reduction after accounting for timing uncertainty, exposure overlap and episode-level dependence.

02Literature review and research gap

Earlier evaluations of GRAP have often compared air-quality indicators before and after task-force directions. Some reported reductions in AQI or pollutant concentrations after GRAP directions, while more recent stage-wise evaluations found that PM2.5 remained high during stricter stages and that improvements depended strongly on meteorology. Many existing evaluations share important limitations:

  1. They often use a small number of monitoring stations.
  2. They may examine only a limited set of pollutants.
  3. They frequently rely on simple before-and-after comparisons.
  4. They may not distinguish policy order dates from actual implementation windows.
  5. They may treat daily observations as independent, although they are clustered within episodes.
  6. They may include escalation and re-entry events in ways that contaminate the comparison.
  7. They may not adequately address confounding by indication.
How should GRAP Stage III/IV be evaluated when the policy is reactively triggered, episodes repeat, exposure windows overlap and pollution observations are autocorrelated?

This study fills that gap with a corrected pipeline that separates clean first-entry episodes from escalations and re-entries, restricts comparison days to periods free of Stage III/IV, and uses uncertainty methods suited to dependent time-series data.

03Research questions and hypotheses

  1. Did GRAP Stage III/IV exposure reduce PM2.5 relative to comparable unexposed days?
  2. Did the estimate remain robust after correcting timelines, exposure definitions, event eligibility and comparison-window contamination?
  3. Was uncertainty properly accounted for, given that pollution days are clustered within episodes?
  4. Did severe pollution episodes persist during or outside Stage III/IV exposure?
  5. What can the revised evidence reasonably support about the effectiveness of Stage III/IV?

4.2 Outcome variable

The primary outcome is PM2.5 on the 1+PM2.5 scale. Effects on this scale are approximate percentage changes in PM2.5, not exact raw-concentration percentage changes.

4.3 Exposure variable

The revised timeline records, for each order: the order date, early and late effective implementation bounds, publication times, source type, verification status and uncertainty notes. This matters because the time an order is issued may differ from when its measures become effective.

05Methodology

5.1 Exposure definition

Exposure was defined using exact interval unions at minute resolution rather than hour-start counting, avoiding errors at partial-day boundaries and overnight transitions. Under late-bound timing, Stage III was in force for 44 days and Stage IV for 38 days. Across timing scenarios, Stage III ranged from 39 to 50 days and Stage IV from 33 to 40.

5.2 Clean comparison pool

clean comparison day = no Stage III/IV exposure anywhere in [d − 7, d + 7]

This keeps treated days out of the control group and reduces bias near policy transitions. The comparison pool fell from 147 to 129 days after this correction.

5.3 Episode-level uncertainty

The confidence interval resamples whole entry episodes with replacement and resamples comparison days in moving calendar blocks (7, 14 or 28 days; 14 is primary). The expected-change model is refitted in every one of 4,000 replicates. This respects the fact that days within an episode share meteorology and emissions and are autocorrelated.

6.2 Why the estimate changed

Correcting the two GRAP start-time boundaries did not change any daily exposure classification, so the estimate stayed at +4.2%.

The dependence-aware bootstrap widened the interval: the original independent bootstrap treated pollution days as independent, which understated uncertainty.

The largest change came from restricting to clean first-entry episodes. The four removed starts had overlapping or already-treated pre-windows, and their raw changes were −23%, +58%, −5% and −22%. Including them mixes escalation and re-entry effects with genuine first-entry effects.

07Interpretation

The +11.7% estimate should not be read as proof that GRAP increases PM2.5. Stage III/IV is triggered in response to severe air quality, so exposed days differ systematically from unexposed days: a classic case of confounding by indication.

After correcting for timeline uncertainty, exposure overlap, event dependence and comparison-window contamination, the study finds no statistically significant short-run reduction in PM2.5 associated with GRAP Stage III/IV exposure.

The wide interval reflects limited statistical power. With only six clean first-entry episodes, the design cannot rule out moderately sized positive or negative effects.

08Policy implications

No naive before-and-after

GRAP is activated during severe episodes, so simple comparisons are biased toward finding higher pollution during GRAP.

Reactive measures may be insufficient

Severe episodes outside Stage III/IV suggest emergency restrictions alone do not prevent extreme pollution.

Source-specific action

PM2.5 comes from combustion, secondary aerosols, regional transport and meteorology. Dust or vehicle measures alone may not be enough.

Regional coordination

Delhi's air is shaped by the wider airshed. City-level restrictions cannot counter regionally transported pollution.

Forecast-based activation

GRAP is largely reactive. Earlier, forecast-based activation could reduce exposure before severe episodes develop.

09Limitations

  1. Small number of clean events. Only six first-entry episodes, so power is limited and the interval wide.
  2. Confounding by indication. GRAP is triggered by high pollution, making it hard to separate policy from episode.
  3. Meteorological confounding. Low wind, inversions, low mixing heights and regional transport can dominate pollution dynamics.
  4. Co-interventions. Winter Action Plans, Diwali, revised schedules and lower GRAP stages overlap with Stage III/IV exposure.
  5. Enforcement quality is unobserved. The study measures policy exposure, not on-ground implementation.
  6. No source apportionment. The origin of residual PM2.5 (stubble burning, vehicles, dust, industry, secondary formation) is not identified.
  7. Scale of the outcome. Effects are on the 1+PM2.5 scale: approximate, not exact, percentage changes in PM2.5.

10Future scope

  1. Use satellite aerosol optical depth and fire radiative power to separate local and regional contributions.
  2. Incorporate meteorological forecasts and reanalysis data for weather-normalised counterfactuals.
  3. Add control regions outside Delhi-NCR for a difference-in-differences design.
  4. Link air-quality data with hospital admissions, respiratory illness and school-absenteeism data.
  5. Use enforcement records: PUC checks, vehicle challans, construction-site inspections, anti-smog-gun deployment.
  6. Develop machine-learning or causal machine-learning models for heterogeneous effects across stations, seasons and weather regimes.
  7. Evaluate whether earlier, forecast-based activation reduces severe episodes more than reactive activation.

11Conclusion

This study finds no statistically significant reduction in PM2.5 associated with GRAP Stage III/IV exposure after applying a corrected, episode-based evaluation framework. The revised primary estimate was +11.7% on the 1+PM2.5 scale (95% CI: −9.4% to +25.5%), with the interval including zero.

The result does not prove that GRAP increases pollution. It shows that the available evidence is insufficient to claim a significant short-run PM2.5 reduction from Stage III/IV measures. The persistence of severe episodes outside Stage III/IV further suggests that emergency restrictions alone may be inadequate against Delhi-NCR's winter air-pollution crisis.

The study's main contribution is methodological: GRAP evaluations must account for uncertain implementation timing, overlapping exposure windows, episode-level dependence, contaminated comparison periods and confounding by indication. Without these corrections, policy-effect estimates can mislead.

Recommended headline statement
After correcting GRAP timing, exposure overlap, event eligibility and episode-level statistical dependence, no statistically significant reduction in PM2.5 was detected during GRAP Stage III/IV in Delhi-NCR. The revised estimate was +11.7% on the 1+PM2.5 scale (95% CI: −9.4% to +25.5%), and severe pollution episodes frequently occurred outside Stage III/IV exposure.
Final one-line contribution
GRAP evaluations must correct for reactive policy triggering, episode dependence and exposure overlap; after doing so, no statistically significant short-run PM2.5 reduction was detected during GRAP Stage III/IV in Delhi-NCR.
